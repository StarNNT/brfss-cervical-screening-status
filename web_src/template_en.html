Cervical screening outreach prioritization tool

This tool supports identification of groups with a higher estimated screening gap in voluntary population outreach. It does not determine cancer risk, a person's true screening history, or which test is needed today.

Within the current interval — approximate survey-derived class Overdue — known prior screening, outside the interval Never screened — explicitly reported in the survey
Scope and safe use
  • For US public-health research and voluntary outreach planning only; the development population was BRFSS participants aged 21–65 who reported no hysterectomy.
  • Do not use for clinical decisions, diagnosis, test selection, or restricting access to care. Do not delay or deny care to people below a threshold.
  • Prior CIN2/3 or cancer, immunosuppression, in-utero DES exposure, symptoms, abnormal results, and other special situations are outside this tool's scope and require clinical guidance and professional evaluation.

Effect of the question change: never-screened rate in 2020 and 2024

Since 2021 BRFSS asks "Have you ever had a cervical cancer screening test?" instead of "Have you ever had a Pap test?". Under the new wording the never-screened share is 8–9 points higher at every age. A cohort aged 45–55 in 2020, of whom 3.9% appeared never screened, appears at 25.4% in 2022 (item without preamble) and 10.8% in 2024 (item with a definitional preamble); the difference is thought to be associated largely with the wording of the item. The model was therefore developed on 2020 data, and the 2024 results describe performance under the changed measurement.

Internal validation and temporal evaluation in 2024

Internal validation: a held-out 20% of the 2020 data ( women). Temporal evaluation: the model refitted to the full 2020 sample with the same hyperparameters, applied to BRFSS 2024 ( women), which used a different screening item. The 2024 columns describe performance under a change in outcome measurement and should not be read as an external validation. Brackets give 95% confidence intervals (1,000 design-based resamples within sampling strata).

In 2024 the never-screened label was measured with a different item; how much of the drop for that class reflects measurement and how much the model cannot be separated with these data, and the 2024 AUC should not be read as a bound in either direction. The overdue class, measured with the timing items, remained calibrated in the large in both years.

Incremental value of additional questions

Real BRFSS 2020 items that the calculator does not use were added to the model in blocks. Each block was tested in its own subsample of women who were asked it: the base model (19 inputs) was compared with base + block on the same folds (LightGBM, 5-fold cross-validation). The difference is the change in mean AUC across the three classes; ± is the standard deviation across folds.

The largest increase in this analysis appears when mammography history is added: mean AUC rises from 0.76 to 0.82 only in the ages 40–65 subsample. This variable was not added to the current form; it was measured in a narrower age range, may proxy access to another screening program, and requires a separate transportability and subgroup-performance evaluation. Mean contributions from the other blocks are close to fold-to-fold variation.

Performance by number of questions

The first nine questions were chosen by adding, at each step, the one that improved the model most; gains become very small after that, so the remaining questions were ordered by their contribution at that step. Selection was done inside the training data; values are from the internal validation set. The bar is the mean AUC across the three classes.

Sensitivity to reporting error

Real answers in the internal validation set were perturbed in a controlled way; the model was not retrained.

Population reweighting scenarios

The internal validation set was reweighted by tripling the weight of a selected group. Predicted and observed rates remaining close in these artificial scenarios shows only sensitivity to these reweightings; it is not evidence of transportability or calibration in a new setting.

Missing-response analysis

Survey respondents who answer "don't know" or refuse differ from others and are more often never screened; the model assigns that survey category a higher never-screened probability. A blank field in this tool, by contrast, usually means that the information is unavailable. The comparison below randomly deletes answers and either assigns the survey unknown category or fills with the item's population mean. Observed rates: never screened %, overdue %.

In the simulation, mean-filling randomly deleted answers produced less calibration drift than assigning them to the survey unknown category. Blank fields in the user form are therefore represented by the population mean; this result is not guaranteed in practice because real-world missingness may not be random.

Methods and limitations

Data. CDC Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS). The 2020 source file contains 401,958 interviews and the 2024 source file 457,670; these are not analytic sample sizes. After eligibility and missing-outcome rules, 98,972 people remained in 2020 and 104,650 in 2024. The 2021–2023 files were not used for modeling: in odd-numbered years screening items were asked in only a few states, while in 2022 the new item without an explanatory preamble raised the never-screened share to 37%; CDC also did not publish its derived cervical-screening variable that year.

Why 2020? In 2021 the question format changed and "Pap test" was replaced by "cervical cancer screening test". Even in 2024 the share of women reporting never having been screened is 8–9 points higher than in 2020 in every age group. The difference is also seen when the same birth cohort is followed (3.9% in 2020, 25.4% in 2022, 10.8% in 2024), and the 2022 excess is present in every state; it is thought to be associated largely with the wording of the item. Overdue rates, by contrast, are similar in both years. The 2020 data, which used the Pap-test items, was therefore chosen for development.

Target population. US participants classified as women by BRFSS, aged 21–65, reporting no hysterectomy (HADHYST2 = 2), and with an outcome label that could be derived reliably. This definition does not replace a current clinical eligibility assessment.

Classes (USPSTF 2018 interval proxy). Up to date: a Pap test within 3 years or, at age 30 or older, an HPV test within 5 years. Never screened: an explicit No to both Pap and HPV. Overdue: a known prior Pap/HPV test that does not meet the current definition. Don't know and refusal responses are not treated as No; records that cannot be classified reliably are excluded from the analytic sample. These are survey-derived research labels, not a status verified from the person's clinical record.

Inputs and missing responses. Variables asked in the same form in both years were used; insurance was harmonized as yes/no and income as eight levels. No variable derived from screening items is an input. During training, an actual survey don't-know/refusal response is a separate category. A field left blank in the user form is represented by the population mean; these two missingness mechanisms are not treated as equivalent. No estimate is produced without age.

Model selection. Logistic regression, decision tree, random forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost and a neural network were compared with 5-fold cross-validation; the three gradient-boosting models were also tuned by random search. Survey weights were used in training and evaluation (the neural network was trained unweighted).

Profiles. Weighted decision trees of depth 4; one for never screened, one for overdue among ever-screened women. Confidence intervals for the rates use strata (_STSTR) and clusters (_PSU) with Taylor linearisation.

Limitations. Data are cross-sectional and self-reported, and screening history tends to be over-reported. Development data are from 2020, so post-pandemic changes may not be reflected. The outcome item changed in the 2024 evaluation, which is not a true external validation. There are no confidence intervals for individual probabilities; subgroup sample sizes and prediction errors are not equal. Findings do not establish causality. Results apply only to the US research population and cannot be transferred directly to other countries, clinical settings, or special-risk groups.
